Algae · Words

Microalgae writing on agar

Year
2026
Medium
Live biological work · agar plate culture · photography and high-resolution scan
Dimensions
Petri dishes, 9 cm diameter; presented photographs variable (typical 60 × 60 cm)
Materials
Selected microalgal strains, BG-11 / f/2 medium, agar, directional LED, controlled CO₂

For an algal researcher, a petri dish is routine. But algae have their own language. On agar they grow into characters, landscapes, and sentences. I do not design the characters in advance; I set the conditions—surface concentration, agar texture, light direction—and let the algae decide what to say. The work is a collaboration between my protocol and their will to grow toward light.

Process · Technique

Each piece is constructed by adjusting three variables: surface cell concentration, agar heating time (which changes texture from translucent to rice-paper matte), and the position of a single LED. Cultures run for 7–21 days. The photographic record is taken under raking light and color-matched to gallery conditions.

Calligraphy has long used living things as its model—the brush follows the wind, the bamboo, the bird in flight. In this work I turn the relation around. The algae are the brush, and the琼脂 is the paper, and I am only the one who prepares the ink and leaves the room.

The first step is to let the filaments embed themselves in the agar. They do not sit on top; they push inside, spread, and make the gel their substrate. The boundary between organism and surface dissolves.

By controlling the surface concentration, I create areas of density and emptiness. Where the algae are thick, the琼脂 becomes dark green and almost opaque; where they are sparse, the light passes through and the surface stays pale. The contrast is not drawn; it is grown.

There is also a small violence in the work. To inscribe a word, the algae must cut into the琼脂 and push outward. The gel tears slightly; the filaments enter the wound. What looks like calligraphy is also an act of invasion and repair.

I use ordinary琼脂， but I control the heating time so that it loses transparency and takes on the texture of yellow rice paper. The medium becomes a page from an old book. The algae respond to this page; they grow differently on matte琼脂 than on glossy琼脂.

Before the琼脂 sets, I pour the algae in. They settle while the gel is still liquid, and as the gel hardens it freezes their first gesture. Everything after that is growth, not placement.

Inside the琼脂， the algae photosynthesize. They produce oxygen, and the oxygen pushes against the gel from within, forming small bubbles. The surface swells, splits, and the word becomes three-dimensional. The work is literally breathing.

This is why I call the work 'living.' It continues after I leave the lab. The characters thicken, fade in the center, climb the rim of the dish, or die back. Each dish is a sentence that keeps revising itself.

The final control is light. A single LED from one side makes the algae grow toward it. I place the light so that one side of the dish becomes dense and the other side sparse. The resulting pattern is not a word I wrote; it is a word the algae wrote while reaching for light.

What the viewer sees is one page. The work is the entire notebook: every version that grew, every word that appeared and disappeared, every bubble that rose and burst. The algae have their own language. I am only the one who learned to provide the page.
