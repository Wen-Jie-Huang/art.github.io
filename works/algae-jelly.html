Algae · Jelly

An unknown alga held in its own thickened garden

Year
2024
Medium
Living culture, gel medium, microscopy, photography
Dimensions
Variable; petri dishes and flasks, 6 — 15 cm diameter
Location
An alkaline lake in Inner Mongolia, China
Materials
Filamentous microalgae isolated from field water, culture medium, agar-based thickener
Edition
Each vessel is unique; photographs edition of 3 + 1 AP

We found this alga in an alkaline lake in Inner Mongolia. Under the microscope it clusters like a peony, a soft green bloom made of filaments. We have not sequenced it yet; for now, it is simply an organism with no name, learning to live in a thickened medium that I prepared for it. The jelly holds it still enough to be seen, yet alive enough to keep growing. I like that tension: the gel is a frame, a garden, and a question at the same time.

Process · Technique

A filamentous microalga was isolated from water sampled in an alkaline lake. Because the cells naturally aggregate into tight granules, the medium was thickened until it held a jelly-like consistency. The work is shown as both living cultures in vessels and macro photographs of the green, granular surface.

Most microalgae I work with are grown in liquid, moving in suspension, countable by turbidity. This one refused to behave like a cloud. It rolled itself into tight, velvet clumps, like green pearls or like the first knot of a plant before it decides to become a stem.

I wanted to keep that clumping quality visible, so I added a thickener to the medium. The result was a transparent jelly with a faint green ring where the algae had settled. The alga could still breathe, still photosynthesize, but it could no longer drift away. It became a resident.

I call it 'Algae · Jelly' because the work is not only about the organism. It is about the meeting between the organism and the substance that holds it. The jelly is not neutral. It changes the light, slows the diffusion of nutrients, and turns every movement of the alga into a slow gesture.

We will sequence it eventually. A name will arrive. But I hope the work keeps some of this namelessness, because the namelessness is also a kind of honesty. We do not know everything that lives in our lakes, and the lakes do not owe us a list.
