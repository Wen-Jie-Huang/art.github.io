Life · Crystallization

A salt lake keeps time in crystals and eggs

Year
2024
Medium
Microscopy, salt-crystal cultivation, dormant Artemia eggs
Dimensions
Variable
Location
A high-salt lake in Inner Mongolia, China
Materials
Brine shrimp (Artemia), salt crystals, brine, microscope slides
Edition
Unique piece + 5 editions

The lake is a clock without hands. It measures life by evaporation: water leaves, salt stays, and the whole surface slowly tightens into mineral frames. Inside those frames, a creature no larger than a breath completes its entire biography in two or three months—then dissolves, and its next generation sleeps on, packed in salt. I am interested in the tenderness of that bargain: a whole lineage agreeing to pause until water returns.

Process · Technique

Specimens were collected from a high-salt lake and observed under a microscope. The work combines living brine shrimp, salt-crystal growth, and preserved dormant eggs in one visual field—so that a single frame contains birth, mating, death, and suspended rebirth.

Artemia, the brine shrimp, is smaller than a grain of sand and almost transparent. Yet in the right brine it multiplies until the water itself changes color, turning the lake a slow, quiet crimson. I think of that redness not as a stain but as a signal: millions of lives briefly agreeing to be visible at the same time.

Their natural life is short—two or three months, a ripple on the surface. When the lake begins to dry, salt crystals emerge like a white architecture around them. The adults cannot survive the crystallization; their bodies become part of the brine's residue. But their eggs are different.

The eggs enter a state called cryptobiosis: life stopped, but not ended. Packed under the salt crust, they can wait for years, even decades. When the water returns, they hatch again, as if the dry years were only a comma in a much longer sentence.

In the mating season, the males perform 'mate guarding'—they clasp a female and swim together, a paired dance that lasts for hours. It looks fragile, almost ornamental, but it is also a strategy against a landscape that could vanish at any moment.

I keep returning to the idea of the splash. A small wave breaks against the salt rim, and in that instant, countless lives touch air and disappear. Every splash is a calendar turning a page.

The title 'Life · Crystallization' tries to hold both states in one phrase: the quick flame of living and the slow geometry of salt. Crystallization is the lake's way of remembering; the shrimp are its way of forgetting and starting over.

What I wanted to show is not only a biological cycle but a kind of faith: that water will come back, that life can be folded into mineral and still remain alive, that the end of one generation is only the closed eye of the next.
